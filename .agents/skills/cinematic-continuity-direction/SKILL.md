# Cinematic continuity direction

This is an original, reusable instruction package. Use when planning or reviewing cinematography and editorial continuity for a cinematic interactive experience. Read the actual vision and approved creative decisions first. This skill does not authorize production, installations, paid calls, or a new creative direction.

## Inputs

Obtain the approved story order, intended emotional development, locations, subject identities, camera references, output aspects, interactive-motion rules, and any restrictions on imagery. Distinguish facts from unanswered preferences. If a creative decision remains open, show labeled alternatives in the next authorized review; do not canonize a convenient technical solution.

For this project, preserve the entire canonical vision, not merely its abbreviated research spine of Big Bang → brains → Earth → real prison → creators within that prison → portal → gallery. The Milky Way, warp, real gate, C7 and other specified beats remain governed by the actual vision. Every creator is physically in the prison. A studio-looking environment cannot silently replace it. Motion must freeze at rest, retrace on reverse scroll, and evaluate directly during fast jumps.

## Build the continuity bible before shots

1. Draw a simple location/axis map: entrances, floor planes, cells, corridors, creator positions, windows, practical lights, and portal plane. Record the camera's approach and exit direction through each space. The map can remain a written specification during a research-only phase.
2. Define the viewer's relationship to the camera: embodied visitor, floating observer, impossible macro camera, or another approved premise. Assign a reason to every reveal, push, turn, and scale change.
3. Establish camera conventions: world axes/units, sensor reference or calibrated projection, focal length or FOV and its axis, aspect/crop, lens shift, distortion, focus distance, aperture, shutter, height, roll, and path. Distinguish camera translation from focal-length change.
4. Establish a light map and exposure reference for each physical space. Specify source direction and apparent size, hardness, color/tint, practical emitters, bounce, atmosphere, reflection sources, and shadow logic.
5. Choose a documented color pipeline. Define input assumptions, working space, view/output transform, output primaries/transfer/range, and expected display class. Do not let independent assets apply unrelated final looks.
6. Define story progress ranges. Give every shot a start/end state, reveal, attention target, legibility region, and transition region. These are positions along user-controlled progress, not a promise that viewers spend a fixed number of seconds there.

## Design each shot as a spatial event

Write a shot card with: what changes, why the camera moves, where the viewer is, which subject controls attention, foreground/midground/background, entry/exit landmark, lens/projection, action phase, light motivation, focus, occluders, and required evidence.

Use spatial staging to create depth: overlapping forms, plausible scale, contact, parallax, light falloff, and atmosphere. Do not create depth by independently sliding flat layers when the intended camera move would reveal their flatness. A constrained 2.5D shot can be valid, but state its permitted camera envelope and test its disocclusions.

Track screen direction and eyelines. Establish the axis of action, and cross it deliberately through a visible camera move or a new spatial establishment when the story requires it. A continuous moving camera is allowed to cross an axis; the point is legible geography, not mechanically enforcing a rule meant for discontinuous coverage. Keep hand/tool contact, body pose, garment state, and action phase continuous through a stitch.

For a creator, show a credible relationship between person, activity, object, and prison surroundings. Decide what evidence makes the prison location legible in the framing. Do not add biographical facts or real identities that the source material has not supplied.

## Make every boundary explicit

Classify it as a continuous rendered move, occluded stitch, match on action, graphic match, dissolve, portal transition, or deliberately visible cut. Do not call all of them a seamless single take.

For a continuous-looking boundary, record both endpoint projection/camera states and match:

- Subject screen position and scale; horizon, verticals, vanishing points, and roll.
- Camera/subject screen-space direction and rate per unit of story progress.
- Foreground parallax and order of occlusion.
- Action phase, pose, hands, props, and garment geometry.
- Exposure, black level, white point, key-light direction, shadow hardness, focus, grain, and optical artifacts.

If independent world scales are joined, declare the scale-reset interval and its concealment. Matching global world coordinates is unnecessary when the cut is fully hidden, but matching the resulting visible projection and light field is necessary. Position continuity alone can still produce a visible velocity change; inspect derivatives of the visible path where a continuous glide is intended.

An occluder must conceal the mismatch across every approved crop and every frame of the stitch. A portal needs a front/back rule, defined entry/exit cameras, crossing threshold, and occlusion behavior. An exposure flare must be motivated and inspectable while paused. Motion blur cannot be the only reason an invalid intermediate frame goes unnoticed.

## Treat film timing as progress geometry

Set all visible states from absolute progress. Shape easing as a pure mapping of progress; do not ease the runtime toward the target over time. Give important recognition moments enough progress distance to be inspectable. A hold can be a region in which a subject remains posed, but it cannot force time to elapse before the next region unlocks.

Do not depend on forward-only surprise, a timed reveal, an audio cue, or mandatory traversal of every intermediate event. Inspect reverse meaning and jump meaning separately. If a boundary only makes sense forward, mark that as a creative problem to solve.

## Review procedure

After production is authorized, inspect a contact sheet at shot starts, ends, quarters, narrative landmarks, and both sides of each boundary. Then inspect the boundary more densely and freeze every suspicious frame.

Compare the same story coordinate reached by forward travel, backward travel, and direct jump. Inspect every responsive crop. Check a long stop: no camera drift, lingering cloth, breathing, dust, flicker, changing grain, or delayed media replacement. Examine the fastest traversal without allowing a queued catch-up sequence.

Use a registration overlay only as an inspection aid: compare horizon, landmarks, silhouette, pose and luminance. A 2D alignment is not proof of matching 3D perspective. Record what was actually inspected and what remains uncertain.

## Output

Produce an approved continuity bible, shot cards, transition contracts, creative-open-questions register, crop plan, and review findings. A shot is ready for production only when its location, camera premise, transition, asset route, and unresolved risks are understandable to another artist.
